Where I need your clinical judgement

Three questions on the drug-interaction project. I will do all the building. What I cannot do alone is decide what counts as clinically serious, and I would rather find that out from you now than defend a bad definition later.

The project in one paragraph

I am building a model that predicts which adverse effects may arise when two drugs are taken together. It learns from a database of post-marketing reports, so what it sees is what got reported, not confirmed clinical outcomes. It works well for effects that are commonly reported, and my actual research contribution is getting it to work on the rare and serious ones, where there is almost no data. That last part is where your input decides whether the work means anything.

Question 1

Is my definition of "serious" defensible?

At the moment a computer rule decides. It searches the name of each adverse effect for words like failure, haemorrhage, infarct, sepsis. If it finds one, the effect is serious. If it finds nothing, the effect is quietly filed as moderate.

That rule matches nothing at all for 9,448 of the 12,725 effects, 74% of them. Every one of those was silently called moderate. Here is what that produces:

Why this matters: my headline result is that the model improves on rare serious effects. If the serious list is wrong, the headline is wrong. An aortic aneurysm rupture sitting in the moderate pile is not a rounding error.

How do you decide, in practice, that an adverse effect is serious? Is there a standard list I should be using instead of inventing my own?

What I do with your answer: the code is already written to accept an expert list and ignore the keyword rule entirely. I have prepared a file of the 771 effects currently counted as serious, plus the ones the rule never looked at. You mark what is wrong, I load it, and every result gets recomputed against your definition rather than my regex.

What I do with your answer: if whole categories should come out, I remove them and rerun everything. This changes every number in the paper, which is exactly why I want to settle it before writing rather than after.
Question 3

Does the same interaction behave differently in different patients?

This is the direction I am most interested in and least sure about. My model treats an interaction as one fixed thing: these two drugs, this risk. I suspect that is wrong, and that the real risk shifts with the patient.

In practice, do you see the same drug combination cause trouble in one kind of patient and not another? Age, kidney or liver function, sex, other medicines they are on. Is there a specific pair where you have seen this clearly?

I am asking because chasing this properly means a lot of work on a much larger and messier data source. If your clinical experience says the effect is real and worth chasing, I will do it. If you tell me the variation you see is mostly explained by dose and kidney function rather than anything the drugs do together, that saves me months.

What I do with your answer: it decides whether this becomes the next study or gets dropped. One concrete example pair from you would give me something to test against.

What I am hoping to leave with

  • A defensible answer on what makes an adverse effect serious, ideally pointing me at a standard rather than a judgement call.
  • A yes or no on whether lab values, device problems and medication errors belong in the model.
  • Your read on whether patient-to-patient variation is worth a study.

None of this needs preparation. If you have time afterwards I will send the marked-up list, but the conversation is the useful part.
